# Holi: A Celebration Close to My Heart

Hosting events is not a walk in the park — especially when you have a month-and-a-half-old baby dictating your schedule. Sleepless nights, unpredictable days, and barely any time for yourself make planning anything feel like a distant dream. But living in the US, I’ve realized that festivals don’t feel like festivals unless you make an effort. Otherwise, the day just comes and goes, and you barely notice. So, despite the baby chaos, I decided I had to host a Holi celebration.

## Childhood Holi Memories: Mustard Oil, Pichkaris, and Lifebuoy Saabun

Holi is a festival incredibly close to my heart, filled with colorful childhood memories. The excitement would start a week in advance as we stocked up on colors—both gulaal and the notorious "pakke colors" that clung to the skin for weeks. My sisters (jiji log) and I would carefully clean our steel pichkaris, ensuring the water pressure was just right. Each of us had a dedicated pichkari with our names engraved on it—yes, even as a child, I had a love for all things personalized!

On Holi morning, we’d drench ourselves in mustard oil—our secret weapon to keep colors from staining the skin too much. A quick breakfast of gujiyas and a glass of milk later, we were ready for battle.

If oil was our shield, Lifebuoy was our biggest threat. The moment someone got hold of a Lifebuoy saabun, we knew what was coming. One by one, everyone was pinned down and scrubbed free of oil, for the color to properly set in. The fights didn’t truly begin until the oil was off!

Cousins would arrive, or we’d head to their place, and the teams would form. Each team had its arsenal—pichkaris, bags of colors, buckets, and mugs. The centerpiece was a massive drum filled with colored water, continuously replenished to fuel our water wars.

Cold? Absolutely. But once soaked, the chill faded, and the game was on. Small kids weren’t spared—they were either dunked straight into the drum or rolled around in the excess colored water on the floor. No one wanted to "waste" fresh supplies on them! 😆

After three to four hours of non-stop mayhem, we’d finally surrender.

## The Great Holi Cleanup: Ubtan, Lifebuoy, and the Battle for the Bathroom

Next came the real challenge—scrubbing off the colors. Mom would prepare a huge bowl of ubtan, the only thing powerful enough (along with Lifebuoy) to remove the stubborn colors. We’d sit in a circle on the terrace, applying layer after layer until little mountains of peeled-off ubtan formed around us. What started as golden turmeric paste soon transformed into swirls of purple, green, and black.

![Childhood Holi Memories: Mustard Oil, Pichkaris, and Lifebuoy Saabun](images/holi-celebration/holi-celebration-01.png)

Then came the bathroom wars. With one bathroom and fifteen people, the queue was a battlefield of its own. The poor kids, of course, got the short end of the stick—handed a bucket and told to bathe on the terrace instead. 😆

Finally, after all the chaos, we’d gather for a feast of pooris and kheer, followed by the deepest, most satisfying nap of the year.

## Hosting Holi in the US: Keeping Traditions Alive

For me, Holi has always been about more than just colors—it's about celebrating togetherness. In the US, where festivals don’t naturally surround you, hosting is my way of recreating the joy, laughter, and chaos of home. This year, despite having a newborn, I was determined to keep the tradition alive. So, we decided on a potluck Holi party with friends.

We began the celebration by applying haldi and chandan to Chotu Krishanji and then putting the same as tikka on everyone — a little nod to the traditional Holi spirit, but in a gentler, more symbolic way.

Let the Games Begin: Holi Quiz and Tambola Madness

What’s a festival without a little team competition? (Clearly, my childhood Holi team battles have influenced me deeply.)

We split into three teams and kicked off with a Holi-themed quiz. The first round had 25 questions—from the legend of Prahlad to regional Holi traditions and even Bollywood Holi trivia. My team won the round (obviously)! There was supposed to be a second round where players picked categories and difficulty levels, but we ran out of time. (Which means, technically, my team still won!)

We also played Tambola, but with a twist! My friend Divya made custom Holi-themed tickets, decorated with cute pichkaris, color bombs, and balloons. Tambola was a hit—perfect for our diverse group, which included a 3-year-old and a 60-year-old.

## A Personal Holi Touch: Handmade Gifts and Homemade Flavors

No celebration is complete without a little gesture of love. I wondered what I could do to make it extra special — and then it hit me. My mother-in-law is incredibly talented at knitting and crochet, so I asked her to work her magic. She made beautiful coasters for everyone in soft shades of pink, white, and yellow. I wrapped each one in butter paper and added a hand-painted tag that read "Happy Holi, made with love." To top it off, I decorated the tags with colorful beads for a bit of festive sparkle.

And speaking of love — Sagar poured his into the kitchen, cooking delicious biryani that stole the show. These days, we’ve been watching MasterChef, and he's been feeling inspired to experiment with new recipes. He followed Chef Ranveer Brar’s biryani recipe, and trust me, it turned out so good — rich, fragrant, and packed with flavor. It was gone in no time!

It felt so heartwarming to share something handmade (and home-cooked!), and everyone absolutely loved it.

## Holi, Then and Now: Different Colors, Same Joy

While my childhood Holis were wild, chaotic, and filled with oil-and-color warfare, the version I now host is more about games, food, and laughter. The colors may be different, but the spirit remains the same—togetherness, joy, and making memories.

Our little one even joined the festivities, dressed in the cutest orange-and-white dhoti and angrakha — seeing him in that tiny, traditional outfit was pure joy! And as I celebrate with him in my arms, I know this is exactly how I want him to grow up—surrounded by traditions, people, and lots of laughter.
